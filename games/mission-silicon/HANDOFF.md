## Where it stands

**Now a web app, being rebuilt to teach first (November 2026).** Rob found the Godot prototype's episodes didn't teach and were hard to follow, and that the game was turning from a traditional game into a learning platform, so Mission Silicon moved to the web: [web/](web/), React 19 + Vite + TypeScript, built with bun (the same stack as `stats/`). It's what `/mission-silicon/play/` serves (`scripts/export_web.sh` builds it), and only the *Pilot* is playable there; the other episodes show as being rebuilt on the game's page. Why the web: crisp readable text that reflows (the 480×270 canvas forced tiny pixel text), a first download of about 140 KB gzipped instead of a Godot engine, it works held upright on a phone (no "turn sideways" card), and the web is good at what the lessons need (live requirement tables, diagrams, voice with captions). How it looks: the story is pixel art (the cold open and cliffhanger as full backdrops, portraits beside every line, Pixelify Sans for names), and everything the player reads to learn is clean cards in Atkinson Hyperlegible Next, with Montserrat ExtraBold title cards. The cast talk the player through it: every line, the search's steps and the verdicts are voiced (`web/tools/voice.ts` records them with ElevenLabs v4 through OpenRouter, chosen by ear in November 2026 because OpenAI's audio model sounded drab, one small mp3 per line in `web/voice/`, named from the speaker and the words so an edited line is recorded again; each character's voice and style are in `data/pilot.json`'s `cast`, and a new or edited line needs its direction in `web/tools/directions.json`, the same words with tags like `[flatly]`), with the words always on screen and a mute button. Words stay in `data/pilot.json`; the rules are in `web/src/model.ts`; `bun run test` runs `model.test.ts` (the Pilot's data proves its lesson, ported from `cases.gd`) and `pilot.test.tsx` (the bot plays it through the real screens, one wrong turn per decision), and CI runs it with `bun run typecheck`. The Godot project below is kept for reference while the season is rebuilt (its episode data and simulation), but it's no longer exported or checked in CI. The game page's screenshots are of the web Pilot; `tools/site_art.gd` paints the old ones and is stale.

**The Pilot plays as an interactive sitcom (November 2026).** Rob found the first web Pilot felt like corporate training ("tap each one"), so it was rewritten as a show you watch and step into. The script is `show` in `data/pilot.json`, a list of scenes made of beats: cut to a set with the cast in place, walk, a line (with an optional "Real term" chip), hand over the envelope or the key, a title card or flash-forward card, a lesson stamp, the whiteboard or the TV switching on, a choice, a widget. `web/src/show.ts` says what each beat does to the stage, `Stage.tsx` draws it (the set, and the cast as full-body sprites that walk, turn, talk and carry props, from `tools/sprites.py`: one generated sheet of four poses per character, cut into a strip), and `Pilot.tsx` plays the beats and fills the dock under the stage with the caption, the whiteboard, the TV, the choice or a widget. The episode: Ulrich makes everyone act out the four boxes (Danesh the phone, Ulrich the server, Gerard the database, Gilroy the AI model) and the player decides who Walter's search goes to; each decision is argued on stage while the whiteboard fills in each option against the requirements, then "everyone turns to you"; a pick that fails plays a flash-forward of Thursday (or a month on) going wrong, its champion says when it would be right, and the board comes back with the miss spelled out; Aleister's no-server plan has the player find the key in the app's code, Gerard's sticky notes are answered by whoever plays that box, and the player questions the one plan line nobody can claim. Thursday and Gerard's record are the old widgets, played with the cast on stage. Saves are per scene. New art: nine sprites and a daytime living-room set (`den`, `tools/art.py`). `model.test.ts` also checks the script (everyone can be drawn and voiced, every choice has one right answer, every wrong one plays something), and the bot in `pilot.test.tsx` watches the whole episode, making one wrong pick per kind of choice. The Godot `scenes/pilot.gd` reads fields this rewrite removed; it was already superseded and isn't run. The game page's screenshots are of the earlier web Pilot and need retaking.

## Decided

- **Audience and goal.** Non-technical people, would-be founders above all, who want to build with AI. They learn enough to name the parts, read an AI's plan, ask for the right thing and spot classic mistakes. The player never writes code; that's what makes it distinct from the other three games.
- **The homage.** Silicon Valley (HBO). Same limits as Curb Your Oxidation: copy format, tone and tropes closely; homage names only; no verbatim lines; no recreation of the theme tune; don't name the show in anything players or viewers see. The planning docs here do name it and its characters. Leave out the raunchy bits and the ethnic-stereotype gags.
- **Stay close to the show.** Drifting into generic startup comedy is the main risk. SHOW.md ties every character to a named counterpart (Erlich, Richard, Gilfoyle...) and every episode to a specific borrowed set piece; keep it that way, and check each episode against the show before building it.
- **The title: Mission Silicon.** The show's title turned around, as Curb Your Oxidation twists its show's. The incubator house is a run-down Victorian in the Mission, "mission" is the tech giant's favourite word, and it sounds like a real neighbourhood. Folder `games/mission-silicon/`, site page `/mission-silicon/`, game at `/mission-silicon/play/`. A web search found nothing else by that name (not a trademark check).
- **The premise.** You're a non-technical founder who moves into an incubator house to launch an AI startup, **Forkcast**, an AI dinner concierge that finds and books restaurant tables. Forkcast is also the curriculum's running example; its stages are the season's arc.
- **The cast.** Winking names, like Curb's (Larry David, Barry Danvers): a similar sound or the same initials, never the real name. Full table in SHOW.md.

- **The AI teammate.** The character the player directs is Aleister Jr., an eager, overconfident AI coding assistant. The finale is the player telling it no.
- **The look: startup green.** An off-white and slate UI with one bright green accent, `#2BB673`, in the vein of the show's startup-logo green. Title cards in Montserrat ExtraBold (OFL, close to the show's geometric sans; put the font files in the game's `assets/fonts/` like Curb's Libre Franklin). Music: original glitchy electronic, bitcrushed synths and a drum machine, never the show's theme. Pixel art like the other games.
- **Landscape**, like the other three games. Phone players are likely (they arrive from social posts), so keep the web build lean, keep text and tap targets big enough for a phone held sideways, and show a "turn your phone sideways" card when it's held upright.
- **The curriculum leads.** It can change for teaching reasons; the show follows it, and every module must stay covered by at least one episode.
- **Engine: Godot 4.7**, its own project in `games/mission-silicon/`, like the others, to reuse the web export, CI, bot tests and art and trailer tools.
- **Notebooks are marimo; React gets its own section.** Both already in the curriculum.
- **The core loop** (details in GAME.md): Aleister Jr. answers each episode's problem with a pull request; the player keeps or cuts each change, asks it the curriculum's "What to tell your AI" questions and sets a few dials, never wiring or writing code; then merges, and a live moment runs simulated diners through what was built. Nothing is marked right or wrong when chosen; only the live moment and Gerard's postmortem judge. Nothing is timed. A failed live moment is a scene, then take two.
- **Runway is the score; time is the plan's budget.** Each episode starts from its own bank and board (no carry-over). Three marks: Live (goal held), Lean (runway at par), Ulrich (his card judged right; he's right about a third of the time).
- **The simulation is a deterministic flow model**, so tests can enumerate every plan. Its numbers are illustrative; its shapes (queueing, cache hit rate and staleness, connection limits, retries without idempotency keys) are real.
- **Each episode's data proves its own lesson**: the solution passes, Aleister's PR as proposed fails, every lesson-bearing decision changes the result, and no plan passes much cheaper than the solution.
